# Positioning and thesis

## Load when

The strategy is generic, resembles a topic list, lacks a controlling idea, or
needs a credible contrast with the status quo or alternatives.

## Questions answered

- What should the audience believe after encountering this campaign?
- Why is this company qualified to make that argument?
- What familiar assumption, legacy approach, or category convention provides
  useful contrast without manufacturing conflict?
- Which few core messages deserve repeated treatment?

## Method

1. Extract the supplied positioning, differentiators, strongest source proof,
   and verified performance learning.
2. Name the audience problem and the conventional response using only supplied
   facts. Prefer critique of an approach or tradeoff over personal attacks.
3. Form candidate theses as `because evidence, we believe implication for this
   audience`. Remove candidates that are merely topics, slogans, or unsupported
   contrarianism.
4. Select one thesis that is specific, useful, defensible, relevant to the
   objective, and capable of generating multiple distinct briefs.
5. Express the differentiated narrative as the change from old frame to new
   frame. Identify two to four recurring messages that reinforce it without
   repeating identical content.
6. Check that pillars, themes, and briefs visibly inherit the thesis.

## Required inputs

Company positioning and differentiators, campaign objective, priority audience,
Nimi moments/angles, and any verified performance or eligible memory used.

## Expected contract effect

One coherent `thesis`, `differentiatedNarrative`, related pillars, themes, and
brief messages. The output remains `ContentStrategy`.

## Failure modes

Consensus summaries, empty provocation, invented competitor claims, false
dichotomies, personal antagonism, coined language without explanatory value,
or disconnected themes.

## Evidence and authority

Interpretation may be original, but its factual premises retain exact supplied
IDs. Competitor, category, or market claims require supplied evidence; skill
examples never supply it. A thesis is a proposal, not an authorization.
